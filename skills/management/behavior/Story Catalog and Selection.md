## Core Concepts

- **The story catalog exists to offload working memory.** In an interview, your working memory is already spent on impression management, reading the interviewer, and structuring responses. Adding "search my entire career" to the stack produces mediocre answers. Solution: identify, organize, and signal-area-map your stories *in advance* so in-interview recall is a pointer lookup, not a search.

- **Two catalog layers: Core stories (3–5) + Additional stories (5–7).** **Core** = your peak-capability projects, multi-signal, the stories you'd tell a hiring manager if you had 5 minutes. **Additional** = gap-fillers that cover any signal area your core stories miss, plus variety when a core story has already been used. The numbers are guidance — the real target is *confidence you can answer almost anything*.

- **Journaling is the surfacing technique.** Don't try to "think of stories" — write. Use five memory-trigger categories: High-impact projects, Challenging situations, Leadership moments, Learning experiences, Career transitions. Projects that keep reappearing across categories are your Core stories. Journaling also helps you *process unresolved emotion* — interviewers detect and harshly penalize raw unprocessed feeling in stories.

- **Story scope must match target level.** Common stories at each level — SWE I: first feature end-to-end, critical prod-bug investigation, automation of manual toil. SWE II: month-long project from scratch, performance bottleneck resolution, tooling adoption. Senior (L5): legacy-to-modern migration, widely-adopted API, technical-debt paydown with leadership buy-in. Staff: multi-quarter technical strategy, major incident response, 50%-velocity platform, org-wide architecture. Senior Staff/Principal: product pivot with team + architecture rebuild, organization scaling, acquisition integration, company-wide practice establishment. Junior-level stories in a senior loop = instant downlevel.

- **Small-company candidates worry their scope doesn't match — usually misplaced.** Skilled interviewers evaluate *signal areas*, not project magnitude. The real work is *articulating* complexity and impact in language that resonates with the target audience. This is a translation problem, not a scope problem.

- **Catalog entry structure:** For each story, capture Context (situation, role, stakes, constraints — stakes are the most important element), Actions (what *you* did; brainstorm across Designing, Aligning, Communicating, Implementing, Iterating, Releasing, Thinking/Deciding), Results (quantified preferred, non-quantified for senior roles is fine — scope growth, people developed, practices adopted, cultural change), Learnings (technical / process / people / strategic), and Signal Areas (which of the 8 this story covers + any company-specific values).

- **Fill coverage gaps signal-area-by-signal-area.** After core stories are identified, go through all 8 signal areas and ask discovery questions: *Scope* — largest project owned end-to-end? Above-level work? *Ownership* — solved a problem not officially yours? Followed through when others moved on? *Ambiguity* — moved forward with incomplete/conflicting info? Shifting requirements? *Perseverance* — serious obstacle? Cancelled/pivoted project? *Conflict* — strongest disagreement with manager? Difficult coworker? *Communication* — adapted for non-technical audience? Miscommunication caused real problems? *Growth* — biggest mistake and aftermath? Feedback that changed you? *Leadership* — influenced without authority? Unstuck a colleague?

- **Memory-trigger techniques when stories are hard to recall:** (1) **Career artifacts** — performance reviews (especially self-reviews), old resume/LinkedIn versions, calendars, presentations, PRDs/RFCs, email threads, code repo commit messages, retro docs. (2) **Interview past coworkers** — "what project do you remember me being most involved in?" "what was I particularly good at?" (3) **Sensory triggers** — revisit past work locations, similar food, music from that period, photos, old swag, technical books you referenced. Unconventional but works.

- **In-interview selection criteria, in priority order: Scope → Relevance → Uniqueness → Recency.** This priority is deliberate and often counterintuitive. Scope trumps Relevance because interviewers want the high-water mark of your capabilities, even if the literal question asks about something slightly different. A higher-stakes conflict with a partner PM beats a minor disagreement with your manager when the question is "disagreement with manager." Relevance trumps Recency because signal fit beats freshness. Uniqueness becomes important as the interview progresses — early questions take your best stories regardless; later questions should rotate to show breadth.

- **Scope dimensions for selection:** Breadth of actions (technical-only vs also planning/comms/stakeholder), timescale (2 weeks vs 18 months), complexity (technical + organizational — how many teams? how many priorities?), business impact (did it move the needle on revenue/performance/users/team?). Don't save best stories "for later" — lead with them.

- **Menu Technique: offer a choice when multiple stories are strong.** "I could tell you about two different approaches to conflict resolution — one about technical architecture disagreement across teams, another about resource allocation with my manager. Which would be more useful?" Benefits: demonstrates you have multiple relevant examples, ensures you deliver the signal they actually want, gives you a breath to collect thoughts. Don't overuse; deploy only when you genuinely have two strong options.

- **When you don't have a perfect story: reframe, don't fabricate.** Get as close as possible to the question, then be honest about your actual role: "The senior engineer made the final architecture calls — I drove the proof-of-concept that informed the approach. Here's what I actually did…" Interviewers want repeatable behaviors, not literal answers. If you truly have nothing, pivot to hypothetical: "I haven't led a project at that scale, but here's how I'd approach it based on my contributions to similar initiatives…"

- **Never lie or embellish.** Three reasons: (1) *character damage* — you'll lose self-respect and seed impostor syndrome even if you get the offer; (2) *lying is operationally hard* — you won't have the detail for follow-ups, and experienced interviewers notice when timelines get vague or confidence drops under probing; (3) *industry risk* — principled rejection or reputation damage. Reframe your actual contribution honestly instead: "I drove the initial POC that informed the architecture" > "I led the migration."

- **Values questions need a framework, not a story.** "What's your approach to cross-team communication?" is a *values* question, not "tell me about a time." Answer with a concise framework (2–3 variations, named, with a one-line "when to use each"), then immediately anchor with an example from your catalog. On-the-fly framework construction: ask "what varies?" (stakeholders / time / resources / risk), pick 2–3 variations, name each, add one insight. Common framework patterns: communication (frequency + formality axes), prioritization (impact vs effort, or value vs risk), conflict (by stakes), quality (speed/quality/scope triangle).

- **Hypotheticals get clarifying questions + pattern-matching.** Ask 1–2 *meaningful* clarifying questions — ones that would genuinely change your approach ("B2B or B2C?" "Do I have hiring authority?" "Who are the key decision-makers?"). Poor clarifying questions: too basic, too numerous, or deflecting. Then pattern-match: abstract the core tension (e.g., "project behind schedule" = resource/time/scope tension), scan experience for similar tensions, extract transferable principles, and ground the answer with a related real story from your catalog.

### Failure Modes by Level (from Hiring Physics research)

- **Staff/Principal → Director: Three failure modes.** (1) *Technical Identity Lock* — "my biggest accomplishment was designing our event-driven architecture at 99.99% uptime." Fix: "the strategic question was whether our architecture could support 10x growth without 10x infrastructure cost." Reframe technical work as business decisions. (2) *Missing Influence Evidence* — "I influenced the team to adopt TypeScript by building a proof of concept." Fix: prepare explicit influence stories with a business case, org alignment, and multi-team execution thread. (3) *Scope Ceiling* — stories describe team/project-level impact. Fix: use the Scope Ladder; upgrade key stories to system-level before the loop.

- **Senior Manager → Director: Three failure modes.** (1) *Team-Centric Framing* — "I'm proud of the team culture I've built." Fix: reframe around org contribution — "I identified that engineering culture variance across teams was creating cross-team friction. I established practices adopted across three teams by demonstrating results, not mandating." (2) *Process Over Judgment* — "we have a quarterly review process for technical debt." Fix: show the judgment behind the process — "the process surfaces candidates; my judgment determines priority based on three criteria: velocity impact, risk concentration, and optionality cost." (3) *Missing Strategic Connection* — every accomplishment story needs a sentence connecting to business strategy ("this project mattered because...").

- **Director → VP: Three failure modes.** (1) *Functional Silo* — "I've built a strong platform org — 50 engineers, clear roadmap." Fix: "I transformed platform from a cost center to a strategic capability. Revenue-per-engineer increased 30% over two years." (2) *Manager of Managers Identity* — "I oversee four teams with six managers; I develop my managers." Fix: "I'm accountable for platform reliability and developer productivity — the managers and teams are how I deliver on that accountability." (3) *Insufficient Executive Presence* — communication style of a Senior Manager in a VP interview. Fix: practice, not awareness. Record and compare to executives you admire. Work with a coach or mentor.

- **Pre-interview preparation: three phases.** *Phase 1 (Foundation, ongoing):* Build story library (10-15 stories), develop Executive Narrative, identify your failure modes, start pressure inoculation. *Phase 2 (Company-specific, 1-2 weeks before):* Culture research on five dimensions, interviewer research per person, story selection (5-7 active rotation), anticipate challenges with RAPID responses. *Phase 3 (Final, 48-72 hours before):* Full mock interview, prepare 3-5 tailored questions, logistics, then *stop preparing* — over-preparation creates anxiety.

- **Strategic questions to ask interviewers (senior-level).** For hiring managers: "What does success look like at 6 months? At 18 months?" / "What's the hardest part of this role not obvious from the JD?" / "What's the most important decision this role will make in the next year?" For executive interviewers: "What's the biggest strategic challenge facing engineering right now?" / "What do you wish engineering did differently?" For peer interviewers: "How do decisions get made when your function and this one disagree?" Avoid generic questions ("what does a typical day look like?") — they signal preparation laziness.

## Key Questions

**Q: How did you prepare your story catalog for this interview loop?**
Answer framework: Walk through the two-layer structure — 3–5 core stories (peak-capability, multi-signal) + 5–7 additional stories for signal-area coverage. Explain the journaling discovery process (five memory-trigger categories: high-impact projects, challenging situations, leadership moments, learning experiences, career transitions). Show you also went signal-area-by-signal-area to fill gaps — "I realized my cores all had strong ambiguity signal but weak communication signal, so I added two communication-forward stories."

**Q: You're asked a question and have two strong stories. How do you decide?**
Answer framework: Walk the priority order — Scope first (which story operates in the larger "box"), Relevance second (which directly hits the requested signal area), Uniqueness third (rotate to avoid repetition as interview progresses), Recency last (recent weighted more at junior levels, less at senior). If genuinely tied, use the Menu Technique — offer the interviewer a one-line preview of each and let them pick.

**Q: How do you handle a "leadership" question when you're an IC without direct reports?**
Answer framework: Leadership signals come from influencing without authority — mentoring, driving cross-team initiatives, representing the team externally. Go back through existing core stories and *surface* the leadership threads rather than inventing new ones. If a technical story already has a "convinced the tech lead," "mentored two juniors," or "ran the design review" moment, that's your leadership evidence — name it explicitly rather than burying it.

**Q: How do you answer "Tell me about your favorite project" — pick by preference or by scope?**
Answer framework: Not preference — by scope. "Favorite project" is not asking about personal affinity the way "favorite movie" does; interviewers want the project that best represents your peak capability. Choose the largest-scope story that's relevant to the target role; if the literal "favorite" story is smaller, mention it briefly as a footnote at the end but lead with your scope story. See also [[Big Three Questions — My Prepared Answers]].

**Q: The question is "tell me about a conflict with your manager," but all your manager conflicts were minor — what do you do?**
Answer framework: Apply Scope-over-Relevance-over-literalness. Pick a higher-stakes conflict with a senior stakeholder — a PM, a partner-team TL, a director — and frame it honestly ("this wasn't with my direct manager, but it was with a senior stakeholder who had equivalent authority over the work"). You still deliver conflict-resolution signal *against authority* with real stakes. Do not fabricate a manager conflict.

**Q: How do you answer a values question like "What's your approach to cross-team communication?"**
Answer framework: Start with a structured framework — e.g., three layers: (1) tight-loop comms with direct dependencies, (2) structured touchpoints with adjacent teams, (3) broadcast comms for the wider org. Then immediately anchor with a concrete example from your catalog — "I used this during the velocity program where layer 2 mattered most because 15+ teams were adjacent stakeholders." Frameworks signal systematic thinking; examples signal you actually do the work.

**Q: How do you approach a hypothetical like "What would you do if a project was behind schedule?"**
Answer framework: Ask 1–2 clarifying questions that would actually change the approach (scope-flex, team-ramp constraints, hard-deadline vs soft-deadline, stakeholder audience). Then abstract the core tension (resource/time/scope triangle), walk through your decision principles (what you'd cut, what you'd preserve, who you'd involve, when you'd escalate), and ground with a real past story that faced the same category of tension.

**Q: You've been asked about a major leadership initiative but you've only contributed to large projects, not led them. How do you respond?**
Answer framework: Reframe, don't fabricate. Pick the largest initiative you contributed to and be explicit about your actual role — "The senior engineer led the migration; I owned the proof-of-concept on the communication pattern that the team ultimately adopted." Explain what you *did* lead even inside the bigger initiative. If genuinely nothing fits, pivot to hypothetical framing: "I haven't led at that scale yet, but here's how I'd approach it based on [nearest experience]." Never lie.

**Q: What are the failure modes for a Director → VP interview and how do you guard against them?**
Answer framework: Three specific failure modes: (1) Functional Silo — describing your org as strong (50 engineers, clear roadmap) rather than framing your contribution as business impact ("transformed platform from cost center to strategic capability; revenue-per-engineer up 30%"). (2) Manager of Managers Identity — describing who you oversee rather than what you're accountable for ("I'm accountable for platform reliability and developer productivity — the org structure is how I deliver that"). (3) Insufficient Executive Presence — communication style doesn't match VP register under pressure. This requires practice, not just awareness.

**Q: How should you structure your preparation in the 72 hours before a senior interview loop?**
Answer framework: Phase 3 is three steps + one stop. Step 1: full mock interview under realistic conditions (video, business attire, no notes, 45-60 min, specific debrief). Step 2: prepare 3-5 tailored questions for each interviewer that show strategic thinking and genuine research — not generic questions. Step 3: confirm logistics, test technology. Then: *stop preparing*. Review your Executive Narrative, active story rotation, and RAPID responses for likely challenges, then close the notes. Confidence comes from preparation already done; over-preparation at the end creates anxiety, not readiness.

## Summary

The Story Catalog is the second step of the Decode → Select → Deliver cycle — it exists so that in-interview recall is a pointer lookup, not a search through your career, freeing working memory for the parts of the interview that actually need it (reading the interviewer, structuring responses, managing impression). A well-built catalog has two layers: 3–5 **Core stories** representing peak, multi-signal capability (the stories you'd lead with if you had 5 minutes with the hiring manager), and 5–7 **Additional stories** filling coverage gaps and providing rotation variety. Journaling is the surfacing technique — writing through five memory-trigger categories (high-impact projects, challenging situations, leadership moments, learning experiences, career transitions) reveals which projects keep reappearing, which are your cores. Gaps are then filled by walking the eight signal areas (Scope, Ownership, Ambiguity, Perseverance, Conflict Resolution, Growth, Communication, Leadership) with discovery questions per area. When stories are hard to recall, memory-trigger tactics include digging through career artifacts (self-reviews, calendars, PRDs, commit messages), interviewing former coworkers, and sensory triggers (work locations, music, photos from the period).

In-interview selection runs a deliberate priority: **Scope → Relevance → Uniqueness → Recency**. Scope outranks Relevance because interviewers are measuring the high-water mark of your capability, not answering the literal question — the largest-scope story that reasonably fits the signal asked is almost always the right pick. Relevance outranks Recency because signal fit beats freshness. Uniqueness becomes more important as the interview progresses — don't save your best for later, but do rotate to avoid repeating stories by question 3 or 4. For senior candidates a standout story from two years ago can outweigh a less impressive recent one; for junior candidates, anything older than a year starts decaying in value. The **Menu Technique** ("I could tell you about two different approaches — one X, one Y — which would be more useful?") is the right move when you have two truly strong options, giving the interviewer agency and you a breath to collect thoughts. When you don't have a perfect story, *reframe honestly* — be explicit about your actual role in a larger initiative, or pivot to hypothetical framing. **Never lie or embellish**: it damages character, is operationally hard to sustain under follow-up probing, and carries industry-reputation risk.

Two question types break the "pick a story" pattern and need their own handling. **Values questions** ("What's your approach to X?") require a generalized framework — 2–3 named variations with a one-line rationale for each — anchored immediately afterward with a concrete example from your catalog. Frameworks can be constructed on the fly: ask "what varies?" (stakeholders, time, resources, risk), pick 2–3 variations, name each, add one insight. **Hypotheticals** ("What would you do if…") get 1–2 *meaningful* clarifying questions (ones that would actually change the approach, not deflection), followed by pattern-matching — abstract the core tension, scan past experience for similar tensions, extract transferable principles, and ground with a real story from the same tension category. For an AI Infra Manager loop, this catalog-selection discipline pairs with [[CARL Framework and Story Delivery]] for the delivery step and [[Big Three Questions — My Prepared Answers]] for the three pre-prepared flagship responses (TMAY, favorite project, conflict).
