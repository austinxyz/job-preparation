# eBay - Embracing a New Leadership Challenge

## Situation

After 15+ years at eBay — 12 of them in cloud infrastructure — I had built a successful Application Lifecycle Management team and was operating in a domain I knew deeply. When my manager asked me to take over the cloud fleet and core services team, it was a genuine stretch: a different technical domain, higher criticality (this team's API server was relied on by every eBay developer), and a team that had been without a manager for 6 months. The team was stuck in constant firefighting mode, customer complaints were escalating, and system reliability had dropped below 90% with critical incidents multiple times per week.

## Task

I could have stayed in my established domain. I chose not to. Taking on this assignment meant navigating technical unfamiliarity while also needing to deliver results fast — the team needed leadership, customers needed reliability, and leadership expected improvement within months. My task was to rebuild team effectiveness and push reliability above 99% without domain expertise to lean on from day one.

## Action

- Accepted the assignment intentionally, treating the unfamiliarity as the learning opportunity rather than a risk to manage around
- Started by learning from the outside in: engaged key customers (eBay developers) to understand their primary pain points before forming any internal opinions about what to fix
- Borrowed expertise deliberately: partnered with the Cloud SRE team to import SRE best practices rather than trying to reinvent reliability engineering from scratch
- Anchored the team's work in customer-defined SLOs, translating vague complaints into specific non-functional requirements and observable targets
- Built the observability foundation the team lacked: dashboards, alert systems, and runbooks for each incident type — giving engineers clear tools instead of relying on tribal knowledge
- Formed a dedicated reliability sub-team (1 lead + 2 members) to concentrate focus on the highest-leverage work
- Introduced post-incident retrospectives as a standing practice; explicitly reframed mistakes as learning inputs rather than failures to punish
- Recognized and celebrated small wins both with the team and externally with customers — visible progress rebuilt confidence faster than any technical fix

## Result

- Service reliability improved from below 90% to stable 99%+ within 2–3 months
- Eliminated major incidents; reduced incident recovery time from multiple days to under one hour
- Team morale measurably improved — retrospectives shifted from blame to learning, and engineers began proactively surfacing risks rather than waiting for incidents
- Customers sent positive feedback and thank-you emails within 2 months — external validation of the turnaround
- Established durable foundations (SLOs, runbooks, alert coverage) that reduced ongoing firefighting load

## Key Questions

**Q: Tell me about a time you took on something well outside your comfort zone.**
Talking points: Name the specific stretch (new domain, leaderless team in crisis, 6 months of deferred problems); explain why you said yes (belief the skills transferred, not just obligation); describe how you navigated the unfamiliarity (borrowing SRE expertise, customer-first learning); outcome shows the bet paid off.

**Q: How do you lead effectively when you don't have deep domain expertise?**
Talking points: Start from customer needs, not technical assumptions; identify who does have the expertise and partner explicitly (SRE team); ask questions before prescribing solutions; use structured frameworks (SLOs, runbooks) to establish rigor without needing tribal knowledge.

**Q: How do you rebuild a demoralized team quickly?**
Talking points: Psychological safety first — celebrate wins, reframe mistakes as learning; give engineers ownership of real solutions (not just task execution); make progress visible externally (customer recognition matters more than manager praise); retrospectives convert firefighting culture into learning culture.

**Q: What does growth mindset look like in a senior leader?**
Talking points: Choosing hard problems over comfortable ones when growth matters more than execution certainty; using unfamiliarity as a discovery mechanism (learning from customers, from SRE team); modeling risk-taking explicitly so the team does the same; treating the stretch as evidence of growth, not exposure.

## Summary

After more than a decade in cloud infrastructure, I was asked to lead a team outside my established domain — one that had been in crisis for months. Accepting that assignment was a choice I made deliberately, treating the discomfort as the point rather than something to be minimized. The technical domain was new to me, but the management challenge — rebuild trust, establish clarity, and make the team feel like they were making progress — was exactly the kind of problem I'd been building toward.

The key judgment call was recognizing what I didn't know and borrowing expertise rather than faking confidence: partnering with the SRE team to import best practices, anchoring everything in customer-defined SLOs rather than internal opinions, and giving engineers ownership of the solutions. Reliability recovered from below 90% to above 99% within three months, and more durably, the team shifted from reactive firefighting to proactive ownership — which meant the results would hold after I moved on. This is what growth mindset looks like at the management level: not just learning new tools, but choosing problems that require you to grow into them.
